About Diwali

Diwali, also known as Deepavali, is one of India's most widely celebrated festivals. Known as the Festival of Lights, it symbolizes the triumph of light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, and good over evil. Families celebrate by lighting diyas, decorating their homes, sharing sweets, and offering prayers. In many North Indian households, Goddess Lakshmi and Lord Ganesha are worshipped for prosperity, wisdom, and auspicious beginnings.

Significance

Diwali represents spiritual renewal, gratitude, and hope. In popular Hindu tradition, the festival is associated with Lord Rama's return to Ayodhya, while other traditions commemorate Lord Krishna's victory over Narakasura. Lighting diyas symbolizes the removal of darkness and the welcoming of positive beginnings. Customs and religious observances vary across different regions and communities.

Diwali Puja Vidhi

Step-by-step worship guidance

  1. 1

    Prepare and Clean the Worship Space

    Clean the puja area, arrange a stable chowki, and gather the worship materials. Rangoli, flowers, and torans are customary decorations, not compulsory ritual steps.

  2. 2

    Arrange Lakshmi and Ganesh Images

    Place clean images or idols of Goddess Lakshmi and Lord Ganesha on a cloth-covered chowki according to your family's practice. Keep a thali, water, flowers, offerings, and a safely positioned lamp nearby.

  3. 3

    Light a Diya and Begin with Prayer

    Light a diya safely and begin with a quiet prayer. Some households also perform purification (achamana) and sprinkle water as part of their established puja method.

  4. 4

    Take Sankalpa (Intention)

    With folded hands, state your intention to worship with devotion and pray for the family's welfare. A formal Sanskrit sankalpa may include the place and date, but its wording should come from a verified local Panchang or priest.

  5. 5

    Worship Lord Ganesha

    Invoke Lord Ganesha first, as commonly practiced, and offer flowers, akshat, or sweets. Durva is used in some Ganesh traditions. You may chant 'ॐ गं गणपतये नमः'.

  6. 6

    Kalash Sthapana (If Followed)

    Where customary, set a clean water-filled kalash with leaves and coconut near the deities. Kalash arrangements and mantras differ; it is acceptable to follow a simpler household method.

  7. 7

    Invoke Goddess Lakshmi

    Offer a prayer to Goddess Lakshmi, then flowers, akshat, kumkum or chandan as customary. In fuller pujas, devotees offer the sixteen upacharas (services); this shorter guide does not replace that formal sequence.

  8. 8

    Offer Incense and Light

    Offer dhoop or incense and show the lamp respectfully, where safe. Avoid heavy smoke in enclosed rooms and keep flames away from fabric and children.

  9. 9

    Offer Naivedya (Bhog)

    Offer fruits, sweets, or family-traditional foods such as khil and batasha. Some households also offer water, betel leaf, or dakshina. Use only offerings appropriate to your own tradition.

  10. 10

    Optional Kubera, Saraswati or Account-Book Puja

    Some families include Kubera, Saraswati, or business account books (Chopda Puja). These are regional or household additions, not universal requirements.

  11. 11

    Chant Mantras and Perform Aarti

    Chant a familiar Ganesh or Lakshmi mantra and perform the household's chosen aarti. The Mantra & Aarti tab includes verified short invocations and clearly labelled Aarti openings, not full lyrics.

  12. 12

    Conclude, Share Prasad and Light Diyas

    Offer thanks, distribute prasad, and place diyas safely if customary. Follow your family's respectful procedure for concluding the puja and clearing offerings. Never leave flames unattended.

Important Guidance

  • This is a simplified household sequence; there is no single compulsory procedure for all Diwali traditions.
  • A complete Shodashopachara Puja includes additional invocations and offerings; use a verified ritual manual or priest for that formal method.
  • Do not copy a city/date-specific Sanskrit sankalpa or muhurat until local Panchang data is independently checked.
  • The materials listed in Puja Samagri are a practical shopping guide, not a claim that every item is religiously mandatory.
  • Use safe lamps and ventilation; never leave lit diyas, camphor, or incense unattended.

Katha (Story) — Diwali

Traditional stories and their significance

Festival Katha

The following is a devotional retelling of Rama's homecoming. The Ramayana recounts Rama's exile, Ravana's defeat, and return to Ayodhya; the specific association of rows of Diwali lamps with that return belongs to widely shared later festival tradition, not a verified quotation from the Valmiki Ramayana.

In a widely cherished North Indian tradition, Diwali recalls the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after fourteen years of exile. Devotees remember the story as a celebration of dharma, hope, and the return of light after hardship.

Rama, son of King Dasharatha, accepted exile in order to honour his father's promise. Sita and Lakshmana accompanied him, leaving behind the comforts of Ayodhya.

During the exile, Ravana abducted Sita. Rama searched for her with the help of Hanuman, Sugriva, and the Vanara allies. In the great battle in Lanka, Ravana was defeated and Sita was reunited with Rama.

After completing the exile, Rama returned to Ayodhya. Bharata and the people welcomed him with joy, and Rama's coronation is remembered as the beginning of an ideal of just rule, often called Rama Rajya.

Popular Diwali retellings describe the people illuminating Ayodhya with rows of lamps to welcome Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana. The lamp-lit homecoming is a cherished festival association, even though the precise Diwali connection should not be attributed directly to the earliest Ramayana text.

Today, many families light diyas, pray, and gather with loved ones to remember the importance of truth, courage, devotion, and generosity. Other communities understand Diwali through different sacred narratives and observances.

Other Traditions Associated with Diwali

Krishna and Narakasura

Naraka Chaturdashi is associated in several traditions with Krishna's defeat of Narakasura. Accounts differ in how they describe Satyabhama's role.

Goddess Lakshmi and Samudra Manthan

Puranic traditions connect Lakshmi with the churning of the ocean; her worship at Diwali is an important household observance in many regions. Do not present a single emergence date as universally established.

Kali Puja

Many Bengali Hindu communities worship Goddess Kali on the Diwali new-moon night. Kojagari Lakshmi Puja is a separate full-moon observance earlier in the season.

Bali Pratipada

The wider Diwali period includes traditions of King Bali and Vishnu's Vamana incarnation, particularly in some western and southern communities.

Jain observance

For many Jains, Diwali commemorates the nirvana of Lord Mahavira; it has its own distinct religious significance.

Sikh observance

Bandi Chhor Divas, observed around Diwali, recalls Guru Hargobind's release from captivity and has a distinct Sikh significance.

Vrat & Traditions — Diwali

Fasting, observances and regional customs

Vrat & fasting practices

  • There is no single Diwali fast observed by every Hindu community. Some devotees observe a voluntary fast or wait until evening puja before eating.
  • Naraka Chaturdashi is associated with early bathing in some traditions, not a universally required fast.
  • Those with health or dietary needs can participate through prayer and other customs without fasting.

Traditional observances

  • Cleaning and decorating the home, making rangoli or kolam, and lighting diyas are common customs, but not compulsory for everyone.
  • Lakshmi-Ganesh Puja and offerings of fruit or sweets are common in many North Indian households; other regions have different primary observances.
  • Sharing sweets, prasad, gifts, and visits expresses goodwill; fireworks are optional and subject to local safety and legal restrictions.
  • Chopda Puja (account-book worship) is followed by some Gujarati, Marwari, and business families.

Regional traditions

  • North India: Many households perform Lakshmi-Ganesh Puja and recall the popular story of Rama's return to Ayodhya.
  • West Bengal: Kali Puja is an important Diwali-night observance among many Bengali Hindu communities. Kojagari Lakshmi Puja is a separate, earlier full-moon festival.
  • Gujarat: Chopda Puja and Bestu Varas (Gujarati New Year) are significant in many communities; the calendar date must be checked for the relevant year and locality.
  • Tamil Nadu and parts of South India: Naraka Chaturdashi may be marked by a pre-dawn oil bath (abhyanga snana), special foods, and family celebration; customs vary.
  • Maharashtra: Vasubaras (Govatsa Dwadashi), Lakshmi Puja, and Bali Pratipada occur within the wider festival season in many families. Gudi Padwa is a separate Chaitra New Year festival.
  • Nepal: Tihar is a related but distinct multi-day festival that honours animals, Goddess Lakshmi, and siblings.
  • Govardhan Puja/Annakut and Bhai Dooj are observed in some regions during the broader festival period. They do not necessarily fall on the immediately following civil day.

Frequently Asked Questions — Diwali

Answers to common festival questions

Questions about this festival

What is Diwali or Deepavali?

Diwali is a festival of lights observed in multiple Hindu and other religious and regional traditions. It commonly represents auspiciousness, renewal, and light overcoming darkness.

Why is Diwali called the Festival of Lights?

The name Deepavali is associated with rows of lamps. Lighting diyas expresses joy, hope, devotion, and the symbolism of knowledge overcoming ignorance.

When is Diwali celebrated?

In many North Indian Hindu traditions, Lakshmi Puja is observed on Kartika Amavasya, with the civil date selected using local Panchang rules. The wider festival includes several observances on different lunar days.

Why is Goddess Lakshmi worshipped on Diwali?

Many Hindu households honour Lakshmi as the goddess of prosperity and auspiciousness. Lighting lamps and offering prayers are ways of welcoming her blessings in those traditions.

Is Rama's lamp-lit return in the Valmiki Ramayana?

The Ramayana recounts Rama's exile and return to Ayodhya. The particular association of rows of Diwali lamps with his homecoming is a popular later festival tradition and should not be quoted as a passage from the Valmiki Ramayana.

What is Naraka Chaturdashi?

Naraka Chaturdashi is associated in several traditions with Krishna's defeat of Narakasura and with early ritual bathing. It is linked to Krishna Paksha Chaturdashi, not a full-moon day.

Which deities are worshipped during Diwali?

Lakshmi and Ganesha are widely worshipped in many North Indian households. Kali Puja is prominent among many Bengali Hindus, and other families may worship Kubera or customary deities.

Is one Diwali ritual compulsory for every family?

No. Rituals vary across communities, sampradayas, and families. A simple devotional puja and a more elaborate traditional puja are both found in practice.

Why do people light diyas?

Diyas express the symbolism of light over darkness and the welcoming of auspiciousness. The number and placement of lamps differ among households.

How do Jains and Sikhs observe this period?

Many Jains commemorate Mahavira's nirvana at Diwali. Sikhs observe Bandi Chhor Divas, associated with Guru Hargobind's release. These are distinct religious observances, not versions of Lakshmi Puja.

Is Diwali associated with the New Year?

Yes, in some communities, including Gujarat. The Gujarati New Year falls around the Diwali period, while Maharashtra's Gudi Padwa is a different New Year festival in Chaitra.

Why are sweets and gifts exchanged?

Sharing food, gifts, and hospitality expresses affection and goodwill. These are popular customs, not universal religious obligations.

Is fasting compulsory on Diwali?

No universal fast applies to all Diwali observances. Some devotees follow voluntary fasting or family-specific food practices; others do not fast.

What is Govardhan Puja?

Govardhan Puja honours Krishna's protection of the people of Braj through the Govardhan Hill tradition. It is generally associated with Kartika Shukla Pratipada; the civil date must be checked for the year and location.

Why is rangoli made during Diwali?

Rangoli or kolam is a customary decorative art associated with welcome, celebration, and auspiciousness. Designs and meanings vary by region.

Why do some households avoid sweeping after Lakshmi Puja?

Some families avoid sweeping after the evening puja as a customary belief about preserving auspiciousness. This is not a universal religious rule.

What is Dhanteras?

Dhanteras is an observance near the beginning of the Diwali festival period. Traditions include Dhanvantari worship, lamps, and in some communities purchasing utensils or precious metals.

Are fireworks necessary for Diwali?

No. Fireworks are optional celebrations and are subject to applicable local restrictions. Diyas, worship, food, and family gatherings are other common ways to celebrate.

What are khil and batasha?

Khil (puffed rice) and batasha (sugar sweets) are offered in some North Indian Lakshmi Puja traditions. Not all households use them.

Are particular foods or clothing forbidden on Diwali?

There is no single rule shared by all communities. Some households follow vegetarian diets or customary clothing choices on puja days; these should be described as local or family traditions.
